The Apollo 13 Myth

The Apollo 13 mission's famous line 'failure is not an option' was coined by NASA's Gene Kranz in a 1995 interview. It was later used in the movie Apollo 13 and became his autobiography title. The phrase misrepresents Apollo's actual engineering approach.

  • Apollo 13's Saturn V rocket had 6 million components with 99.9% reliability. This meant 6,000 potential failures per successful mission.
  • Margaret Hamilton's team developed Apollo's software with a systems engineering approach. They integrated software, people, and hardware from the start.
  • The Apollo program's definition of 'done' included landing on the moon, returning safely, and completing quarantine procedures.

Apollo 12's Lightning Crisis

Apollo 12 faced a lightning strike during launch. Engineers used a backup system to restore power and continue the mission.

  • John Aaron identified that lightning had disabled the dashboard's signal conditioning equipment.
  • Astronauts switched the SCCE to 'auxiliary' mode, restoring all systems and allowing the mission to continue.
  • This demonstrated Apollo's built-in redundancy for every critical system.

Apollo 13's Redundancy

Apollo 13's success relied on multiple redundancies. The mission's objective shifted from landing on the moon to safely returning astronauts.

  • The Saturn V rocket had five engines. One failed to ignite at launch, but four remained operational.
  • Backup crews existed for every mission. Apollo 13 had a backup crew ready if needed.
  • The launch escape system could have saved astronauts if the rocket exploded on the pad.

The Space Shuttle Era

NASA's Space Transportation System (space shuttle) used validated Apollo technology alongside new innovations like fly-by-wire systems.

  • Computers can fail in three ways: immediate error messages, timeouts, or deceptive outputs like fake news sites.
  • The shuttle's guidance system required validation against trusted sources to detect errors.
  • Apollo's success was followed by public and congressional fatigue over costs and limited returns.

Systemic News Failure

When multiple devices show conflicting online news, it indicates a systemic failure. Trusting digital sources becomes unreliable when devices display inconsistent results.

  • Devices may show different news like 'SpongeBob invades Luxembourg' on a laptop, 'Mexico one Eurovision' on a phone, or 'NASA's found Brexit opportunities on Mars' on a tablet.
  • This simultaneous failure across devices means digital news systems cannot be trusted for accurate information.
  • For reliable world news during such failures, use offline sources like newspapers or TV.

Space Shuttle Computer Redundancy

The space shuttle used five IBM AP101 mainframes for critical systems. Four ran identical software for fault detection, while the fifth used a different implementation to handle rare failures.

  • Four identical computers provided redundancy; if one failed, the system could isolate it.
  • The fifth computer ran different software from a separate supplier to prevent identical failures.
  • If all four failed simultaneously, the fifth system would likely still function correctly.

Risk Management in Space Shuttle Design

The shuttle program included a sixth spare computer for in-orbit replacement, but it was removed after early missions due to cost concerns.

  • Spare computers were removed to save payload weight and cost, despite their safety value.
  • Removing spares reflects human tendency to underestimate risk when failures don't occur.
  • This mirrors how people overestimate risks of unfamiliar activities like flying versus familiar ones like driving.

Bathtub Curve in System Reliability

Engineered systems follow a bathtub curve: high failure rates when new, stable middle phase, and increased failures at end-of-life.

  • Early failures in systems like Windows Vista are fixed over time, leading to more stable versions like Windows 7.
  • Late-life failures occur when maintenance shifts to new systems, neglecting existing ones.
  • Berlin Brandenburg Airport delayed opening until 2020 due to neglecting the old system while focusing on the new one.

Challenger Disaster Risk Factors

The Challenger disaster resulted from gradually decreasing safety standards for launch temperatures, despite known risks.

  • NASA managers accepted lower temperature thresholds after previous successful launches at higher temperatures.
  • Richard Feynman's investigation identified mismanagement and risk attitude as primary causes.
  • The certification criteria used decreasing strictness, accepting repeated risks as safe.

Seat Belts and Cloud Systems

Seat belts aren't for driving safely but for crashes. Similarly, cloud systems aren't built for constant uptime but for recovery when failures occur. Modern cloud platforms let us scale instantly, reducing the need for complex tolerance engineering.

  • Cloud services like AWS let you spin up servers in seconds. This is like having fire extinguishers delivered in 30 seconds when smoke appears.
  • Systems can handle regional outages with distributed load balancers and active-active replication. North America going offline might not stop service.
  • The biggest cloud failure point is often the MasterCard limit on an Amazon account. If payment fails, all services stop immediately.

Post Office Horizon System Failure

The Horizon system cost 'something like 10 billion pounds' and was rolled out across 10,000 post offices after a pilot on half a dozen branches. Discrepancies between cash and system records led to wrongful criminal charges against staff.

  • Postmasters were accused of stealing money when the system reported missing funds. Many remortgaged homes or cashed pensions to pay back alleged shortages.
  • Fujitsu claimed Horizon was 'fundamentally sound' despite known issues. The Post Office ignored evidence of system errors for years.
  • A judge called the Post Office's defense 'the 21st century equivalent of maintaining the Earth is flat.' Convictions were overturned after 20 years.

User Experience and System Design

Systems must account for real-world constraints. Two-factor authentication fails for users without mobile signal, and users find creative workarounds for system limitations.

  • A user in a Victorian house with no mobile signal had to run to a hill to get a two-factor code. Developers wouldn't call this a software bug.
  • Microscope companies prevented email attachments to avoid espionage. Field engineers used hotmail drafts to send firmware updates without sending emails.
  • Users will find creative solutions within constraints. A system's success depends on how well it handles these unexpected workarounds.

Accepting System Failures

No system survives contact with reality. Developers must accept that failures will happen and focus on recovery, not blame. The Horizon case shows the cost of ignoring potential errors.

  • When users report system errors, check logs first. Don't assume the user made a mistake. The Post Office wrongly blamed staff for system errors.
  • Tesla's autopilot struggles with traffic lights in unexpected locations. Real-world surprises will always challenge software design.
  • Engineers should design for failure, not assume everything will work perfectly. The Horizon case cost lives and livelihoods due to arrogance.

Dog Detection to Art

A neural network trained to identify dogs can be repurposed by reversing its input-output flow, creating unexpected art through 'deep dreaming.'

  • The model initially claimed 'no dogs' in a venue photo, failing to detect features humans recognize.
  • Reversing the model turns it into a 'dog amplifier,' enhancing dog-like features in any image.
  • This process generated 'deep dreaming,' a new art form from user experimentation with the model.

Coca-Cola to Vodacom

In Africa, Coca-Cola's roadside presence evolved into Vodacom's mobile infrastructure, showing how communication systems adapted to local needs.

  • Coca-Cola's red-and-white huts appeared every 40-50 minutes along African roads in the 1980s.
  • By the 2000s, these same huts still existed but displayed Vodacom branding instead of Coca-Cola.
  • Vodacom's success relied on mobile networks covering large areas with minimal cable, unlike traditional telecoms.

Mobile Infrastructure Challenges

Traditional telecoms failed in Africa due to low population density, unbanked populations, and unstable governments.

  • Successful telecoms require population density, people who can pay for services, and stable governments.
  • Most of Africa had sparse populations, unbanked people living on cash, and high corruption risks.
  • Copper cable theft was common, making physical infrastructure unreliable in rural areas.

Mobile Money Revolution

Users created a digital currency system using phone credit before formal banking existed, which Vodacom and Safaricom later adopted.

  • Prepaid phone credit became a currency transfer method: users sent top-up codes instead of physical cash.
  • This system avoided banks entirely, using phone credit as intrinsic currency for money transfers.
  • Vodacom initially saw this as a 'disaster' since users weren't making calls but texting credit transfers.
  • They later turned it into M-Pesa, Kenya's de facto banking system serving a quarter-million families.

User Ingenuity Over Design

Kenyan users redefined mobile phone usage, creating a digital economy that Vodacom and Safaricom adapted into M-Pesa.

  • Users initially used phone credit transfers for money, not calls, prompting Vodacom to adapt their service.
  • M-Pesa became Kenya's primary banking system, helping a quarter-million families escape poverty.
  • Romania rejected M-Pesa in 2014, preferring Visa, while Afghanistan adopted it for police salaries.
  • Afghan police discovered 60% of 'officers' on payroll didn't exist, showing M-Pesa's impact on accountability.

Failure as an Inevitable Option

The speaker emphasizes that failure is always an option, not something to be avoided. This perspective shifts focus from preventing failure to planning for it. It's a natural part of system behavior.

  • Systems should fail safely and predictably. This prevents cascading failures that could disrupt entire services.
  • Failure modes must be documented and understood. Teams need clear visibility into how systems fail under stress.
  • Automated recovery mechanisms should trigger on failure detection. This reduces manual intervention during incidents.

Practical Implementation

Implementing failure as an option requires technical practices like circuit breakers and retries. These maintain service availability during partial failures.

  • Circuit breakers prevent overloading downstream services. They halt requests when failure rates exceed thresholds.
  • Retry mechanisms should include exponential backoff. This avoids overwhelming systems during transient failures.
  • Fallbacks provide alternative functionality when primary services fail. This maintains user experience during outages.

Cultural Shift

Adopting failure as a natural part of system behavior requires cultural change. Teams must shift from blame to learning after incidents. This builds a more resilient organizational mindset.

  • Post-mortems should focus on systemic causes, not individual blame. This encourages transparency and continuous improvement.
  • Teams should regularly simulate failures. Chaos engineering exercises help teams understand failure patterns.
  • Failure metrics should be tracked alongside success metrics. This provides balanced insights into system health.
