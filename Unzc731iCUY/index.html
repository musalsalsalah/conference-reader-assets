How to Speak

Why Communication Skills Matter

Patrick Winston argues communication skills are essential for success. Students should not face life without speaking and writing abilities. Success depends on speaking, writing, and idea quality in that order.

  • The Uniform Code of Military Justice requires weapons for soldiers in battle. Winston suggests a similar standard: students must have communication skills before entering life.
  • Success comes from knowledge, practice, and talent. Knowledge and practice matter more than innate talent, which is small in the formula.
  • Winston's skiing example shows knowledge (K) and practice (P) outweigh talent (T). A novice skier with K and P outperforms a skilled athlete with only T.

Rules for Effective Speaking

Winston sets clear rules for speaking engagements. No laptops or phones are allowed to prevent distraction. Humans have one language processor, so multitasking reduces focus and performance.

  • Laptops and phones distract the speaker and audience. Studies confirm this, and visible distractions make the speaker perform worse.
  • The rule ensures all attendees stay engaged. It prevents people from missing key points due to distractions.
  • Winston insists on full room lighting to keep audiences alert. Dim lighting signals sleep time, making it harder to stay focused.

Starting a Presentation

Avoid starting with a joke. People are adjusting to the speaker's voice and pace. Instead, make an empowerment promise: state what the audience will learn.

  • An empowerment promise explains the value of the talk. Example: 'By the end, you'll know speaking techniques that could get you a job.'
  • Jokes often fall flat because the audience isn't ready. They need time to settle into the speaker's style first.
  • The promise should be specific and focused on the audience's benefit, not the speaker's agenda.

Presentation Techniques

Winston shares key techniques for effective speaking. Cycling on a topic, building fences around ideas, and using verbal punctuation help maintain audience focus.

  • Cycle on topics three times. About 20% of the audience may not understand at first. Repeating ensures everyone grasps the point.
  • Build fences around ideas to distinguish them from others. Example: 'My algorithm is linear, unlike Jones's exponential one.'
  • Use verbal punctuation to signal transitions. Say, 'First, we'll discuss how to start. Second, we'll cover cycling.' This helps distracted listeners refocus.
  • Ask questions to engage the audience. Wait seven seconds for answers. Questions should be neither too obvious nor too hard.

Optimal Time and Place

Choose 11 AM for lectures. Most people are awake, not fatigued from meals. Well-lit rooms prevent drowsiness. Arrive early to check the venue for surprises.

  • 11 AM avoids post-meal fatigue. People are alert and ready to learn.
  • Well-lit rooms keep audiences awake. Dim lighting triggers sleep responses in humans.
  • Visit the venue beforehand. This avoids surprises and helps address challenges like seating or lighting.
  • Aim for a room that's more than half full. Too empty suggests disinterest; too full causes discomfort.

Tools for Speaking

Winston prefers blackboards over slides for teaching. Boards offer graphic quality, match speaking speed, and provide a target for hands.

  • Blackboards allow visual aids that match the pace of speaking. Slides are too fast for audience absorption.
  • Writing on a board is slow enough for people to follow. Slides often move too quickly for comprehension.
  • A board serves as a target for hands, preventing nervous gestures like hiding hands in pockets or behind the back.

Hand Position and Board Use

Keep hands visible to avoid appearing to conceal weapons. Pointing at the board during lectures is effective even if unrelated to content, as demonstrated by Seymour Papert's constant pointing during his talks.

  • Avoid hands in pockets or behind back to prevent weapon concealment concerns.
  • Pointing at the board creates engagement regardless of content relevance.
  • Papert's technique showed physical gestures enhance audience connection without needing content alignment.

Props in Teaching

Props like the potbellied stove in Hedda Gabler build tension and signal key moments. Seymour Papert used a spinning bicycle wheel to demonstrate physics concepts effectively.

  • Ibsen's play used a stove with glowing embers that grew hotter as the plot intensified, symbolizing the manuscript's fate.
  • Papert's bicycle wheel demonstration used duct tape on a wheel segment to clarify rotation direction.
  • Lazarus demonstrated energy conservation using a steel ball pendulum in 8.01, with the ball gently kissing his nose after release.

Why Props Work

Props activate mirror neurons, allowing audiences to mentally mimic actions. This creates deeper engagement than slides or static images can provide.

  • Mirror neurons help audiences feel like they're writing on a board or moving a physical object.
  • Slides don't allow for this physical connection, making them less effective for teaching.
  • Students consistently prefer chalkboards over PowerPoint for better engagement.

Slide Design Principles

Slides should support speaking, not replace it. Too many words or slides distract from the speaker. The ideal slide has minimal text for clarity.

  • Avoid reading slides aloud; audiences can read themselves and will be annoyed.
  • Remove background elements, logos, and titles to reduce visual clutter.
  • Use 40-50 point font size for readability; smaller fonts encourage excessive text.

Laser Pointer Misuse

Laser pointers cause loss of eye contact and audience engagement. Pointing at slides requires turning away from the audience.

  • Using a laser pointer makes speakers lose eye contact with the audience.
  • Pointing at slides creates a 'tennis match' between speaker and screen, distracting from the message.
  • A better approach is to use physical gestures or direct pointing without turning away.

Slide Design Crimes

Avoid overly dense slides. Print presentations on paper to check for excessive text, insufficient white space, or lack of imagery. This reveals 'too-heavy' slides that overwhelm audiences.

  • Too-heavy slides use small fonts to cram content. Example: A slide with dense text from the 1957 AI conference proposal.
  • Use air and imagery. Example: A presentation with mostly pictures and only 3-4 text slides, each given time to read.
  • Hapax legomenon slides are rare. One per presentation only, like a slide that's hard to understand but makes a point.

Audience Engagement

Audiences disengage during long presentations. Example: A speaker on slide 80 of 100, while the sponsor reads email and a co-sponsor checks lunch menus.

  • Avoid slides that cause boredom. Example: A 80th slide titled 'first of 10 conclusions' with audience members yawning or distracted.
  • Start with a promise. Example: Begin a lecture by showing a cool resource allocation example with colorful U.S. states.
  • Express passion. Example: Highlight how a program that would take longer than the sun's lifetime now runs in seconds.

Inspiring Presentations

Inspire through stories and passion. Freshmen cite high school teachers; senior faculty cite new problem perspectives. Everyone responds to visible passion.

  • Connect to excitement. Example: AI lectures naturally express enthusiasm about cool ideas, making topics engaging.
  • Use historical context. Example: Reference the 1957 AI conference proposal to explain historical significance.
  • Avoid vague claims. Example: Don't say 'teach people to think' without explaining how through stories and analysis.

Oral Exam Tips

Prepare for oral exams by situating research and practicing with unfamiliar audiences. Avoid relying on faculty who know your work.

  • Situate research. Example: Explain how your problem fits global efforts, noting past 30 years of no progress.
  • Practice with strangers. Example: Have friends who don't know your work critique your talk to avoid 'hallucinations' about content.
  • Target older examiners. Example: Older committee members are less critical, making exams easier.

Job Talk Strategy

Job talks must establish vision and accomplishments within five minutes. Example: A candidate must show a problem they care about and a new approach quickly.

  • Define vision. Example: Problem: Understanding human intelligence. Approach: Comparing humans to chimps and Neanderthals on symbolic thinking.
  • Highlight uniqueness. Example: Humans are symbolic creatures, enabling symbolic descriptions of relations and events.
  • Avoid delays. Example: Failing to state vision or accomplishments in five minutes means losing the job opportunity.

Crafting Technical Stories

Technical talks should frame ideas as actionable steps, not just achievements. This approach clarifies your process and makes contributions tangible. It mirrors how engineers validate understanding through implementation.

  • List steps needed to solve a problem, even if you haven't completed all of them. This establishes a clear path forward.
  • Example: Specify behavior, enumerate constraints, implement a system. These steps form a logical framework for your work.
  • Conclude by mirroring these steps with your specific contributions. This ties your work directly to the problem-solving process.

Building Recognition

Recognition matters because ideas deserve visibility. Julia Child's experience at a Venice fundraiser showed how being recognized feels better than being ignored. Your work needs packaging to stand out.

  • Create a symbol for your work, like the arch in the speaker's PhD thesis. This visual anchor makes ideas memorable.
  • Develop a slogan that captures your work's essence, such as 'one shot learning' for a system that learns from single examples.
  • Include a surprise element: challenge assumptions, like learning from one example instead of many.
  • Focus on a single salient idea that stands out, such as 'near miss' for a non-arch structure that's almost correct.

Ending Strong

End with a contribution slide, not generic thanks. This reinforces your work's value and avoids diminishing your impact. Final slides should mirror your talk's structure.

  • Avoid listing collaborators on the final slide. Place them on the first slide to acknowledge contributions early.
  • Never end with 'Thank you' or 'Thank you for listening.' This implies the audience stayed out of politeness, not interest.
  • Use a strong closing like a benediction (e.g., 'God bless you, God bless America') or a personal salute to the audience.
  • Salute the audience by expressing appreciation for their time and engagement, such as 'It's been great fun being here at MIT.'

Welcome and Purpose

The speaker acknowledges the audience's presence as a sign of their recognition that presentation matters. This validates their interest in effective communication techniques. The speaker encourages returning and bringing others to continue learning.

  • Audience presence shows understanding of presentation importance.
  • Speaker thanks attendees for demonstrating this awareness.
  • Invitation to return and bring friends for further learning.
